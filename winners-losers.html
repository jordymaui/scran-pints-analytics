THE NEXT BETTER UPLOAD

Winners & losers.

What to use. What to avoid. How to make the next title and thumbnail stronger.

Our main-video playbook · Saved 4 Oct 2026
TitlesThumbnailsDo & avoidThe Leaderboard Individual clicks. Verified scores.
LEAN INTO

A clear food hook. A reason to care.

A specific price, a recognisable dish or a question people want answered.

MOVE AWAY FROM

Just telling people we did a review.

“We rated it” is the activity. The food, the stakes and the surprise are the hook.

Promising patterns, not magic formulas. Our recorded comparisons point in these directions, but none named a clear winner. Titles and images changed together. The “why” is our interpretation, not a proven cause.

THE WORDS

Good titles sell the question.

Make someone think “I want to know the answer”, not just “they went to a match”.

Our starting recipe

One clear food promise + one unanswered question.

The club name is optional. The stadium-food story is not. Use the strongest truthful hook in the episode, not the same wording every week.

What good and weak titles look like

Writing examples drawn from our used titles. “Stronger” is the direction to borrow, not a guaranteed click result.

WEAKER: THE ACTIVITY
“We Rated Fulham’s Stadium Food & Pints”

Clear subject, but little reason to choose this review over another.

STRONGER: THE STAKES
“Fulham Charged £8.50 for This Stadium Hot Dog?!”

A real item, a specific price and an obvious question: was it worth it?

WEAKER: THE FORMAT
“I Rated the Coventry City Stadium Food out of 10”

The scoring system is not the main reason a new viewer cares.

STRONGER: THE QUESTION
“Does COVENTRY Have England’s BEST Stadium Food?”

Sets up a contest and leaves the verdict open. It is a question, not a claim that Coventry is number one.

THE PICTURES

Make the food story easy to read.

The clearest direction to try again: one obvious food, one reaction, one stadium cue. Here is the actual Fulham comparison.

What the result says: the hot-dog package earned more watch time in this comparison. What it does not say: that the image alone caused it. The title changed too, and there was no clear winner.

Give the eye one main job.

The hot dog is instantly recognisable. Our read: that makes the food and value question easier to grasp than several competing clues.

Make the reaction mean something.

The sceptical expression and pointing hand connect to the food. For future artwork, use a truthful reaction from the episode.

Keep the stadium context.

One recognisable ground cue is enough to establish where we are. The thumbnail should still sell a stadium-food episode.

Our next-thumbnail starting point

Food first. Reaction second. Venue third.

Let the title add the question or price. The image should help people understand the promise, not repeat every word.

Production recommendation, not a proven winning template. Check the real food, face, price and venue against the footage before using it.

Do not turn “blur loses” into a rule.

Hull and Manchester City provide useful counterexamples.

Likewise, Ipswich’s highest-share pairing used a prominent Ed Sheeran face. We cannot honestly say celebrity faces always fail. Judge the whole promise, not one design trick.

THE PRACTICAL BIT

What to do. What to avoid.

Use this before approving the next upload. These are our working creative rules.

Do more of this

  • Pick one question, surprise or value judgement.
  • Use the actual dish or price when it makes the story stronger.
  • Make the food and reaction readable at phone size.
  • Keep the title and image telling the same story.
  • Deliver that promise early in the episode.
  • Change one thing at a time when you want to learn what helped.

Avoid this

  • Relying on “we rated…” as the whole hook.
  • Adding BEST, WORST or SHOCKING without a clear story behind it.
  • Hiding both the food and the verdict by default.
  • Making food, badge, pint, score and arrow all compete for attention.
  • Using an invented price, wrong competition or implied celebrity appearance.
  • Calling the highest percentage a proven winner, or blaming the thumbnail for every viewing drop.

The next brief, in one sentence

Show the most interesting real food moment, give it one clear question or specific stake, and make the picture easy to understand on a phone.

Then check both clicking and viewing. A click that leads straight to disappointment is not the result we want.
Evidence & limits

Based on the five main videos and recovered title/image pairings in this dashboard. No Shorts. Saved Studio data from 4 October 2026, not a live feed.

Fulham’s later comparison ran 17–20 September; Ipswich 23–29 September; Coventry 30 September–3 October. All reported no clear winner. Their watch-time shares describe title-and-image combinations within each comparison, not variant CTR and not a ranking across unrelated videos.

Hull has video analytics but no recovered comparison outcome. City’s version shares are withheld by YouTube for insufficient impressions. Whole-video CTR covers the video’s packaging history, audience and time since publication. It cannot identify which visual element caused a result.

The explanations and do/avoid guidance are editorial recommendations. Price, league, guest and recipe claims still need to be true for the specific episode. Celebrity-linked food does not mean a celebrity appeared in the video.
